# Image quality on the Steam Frame: aliasing, shimmering chrome, and what it costs to fix

*2026-10-02, branch `foveated-rendering`. Measurements on the Steam Frame (Adreno, Turnip Mesa 26.3.0-devel, SteamVR 2.17.10) with the
Ghostbusters VR room table at the native 2160×2160 per eye.*

## 1. Summary

Compared with the same tables on a PSVR2 driven by a PC, the Steam Frame build shows two artifacts: diagonal lines are stair-stepped, and
the shiny chrome parts (ramps, wire guides, metal posts) seem to move or sparkle while the head is still. Neither comes from a feature
missing on the Frame: every visual setting is at the same default as on the PC, and the build has no platform-specific reduction. The
difference is **supersampling**. On a PC, SteamVR's recommended render resolution is well above the panel, and the compositor's
downsampling anti-aliases the image for free; on the Frame the recommended resolution is *below* the panel (1728²) and the branch renders
1:1 (2160²) with no anti-aliasing at all. The raw edges give the stair-steps; the highlights thinner than a pixel, resampled every frame
by the compositor with a head pose that is never perfectly still, give the sparkle.

The anti-aliasing options were measured; only Standard FXAA fits within the 72 Hz budget on a heavy table, and it does not address the
sparkle, which is shading aliasing rather than edge aliasing. Two changes follow:

- **Standard FXAA is the default of the headset builds** (`Player/FXAA = 2` under `__STANDALONE__ && ENABLE_XR`): +1.6–2.0 ms at
  2160² on a cool GPU (+2.5 ms measured in the hot series of section 4).
- **Specular anti-aliasing in the material shader**: the roughness of the glossy lobe is widened by the variation of the normal inside
  the pixel, and the mirror lookup of the specular layer reads the environment map at the mip level matching the footprint of the
  reflection. This targets the sparkle directly, at a negligible cost, on every platform.

## 3. Why the PC looks cleaner

SteamVR on a PC sets the recommended per-eye resolution from the GPU's headroom ("auto" resolution), typically 1.3–1.5× the panel per
axis for a PSVR2 on a capable card, i.e. 2–2.5× the pixels, and the compositor downsamples the eye image to the panel. The application
does not know it is supersampled: it just renders at the recommended size. This is the anti-aliasing the PC image has and the Frame
image does not.

On the Frame, the recommended resolution is 0.8× the panel (the runtime's performance target; Valve's Verified bar is 72 fps at 1728²).
The branch renders at the panel resolution instead, because the sharper image is worth the 56 % more pixels (`ResFactor = 0.2637`), but
that is still exactly one sample per panel pixel. Two consequences:

- **Diagonals**: plain geometry aliasing, one sample per pixel, no filter.
- **Chrome "moving"**: the highlights of the glossy and mirror layers on curved metal are thinner than a pixel. A sample hits or misses
  them depending on sub-pixel position, and the compositor re-projects the eye image every frame with the latest head pose, which
  always drifts by a fraction of a pixel. The pattern of hits changes every frame, so the highlight flickers while the scene is still.
  With 2+ samples per pixel averaged, the same highlight is a steady, slightly wider line. This is *shading* aliasing: edge
  anti-aliasing (MSAA) does not touch it, and a post filter (FXAA) only smears it.

## 4. Cost of the anti-aliasing options on the Frame

Method: `/tmp/aa-experiments.sh` on the headset (the commands are reproduced in section 9). Each variant edits the ini, plays the
Ghostbusters VR room table, waits for frames (the counter reads 0.18 ms until the headset is worn), measures 40 s of the runtime's app
GPU time (`XR_META_performance_metrics`, enabled with `VPX_XR_METRICS=1`), then restores the defaults. Values are the mean of six
5-second samples; the budget at 72 Hz is 13.9 ms. The seven launches ran back-to-back and heated the device (section 6.3): the later
rows are upper bounds.

| Variant | ini keys | GPU per frame | vs baseline | Within 72 Hz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline (no AA), 2160² | — | **9.7 ms** (9.4–9.9) | — | yes |
| Standard FXAA | `FXAA = 2` | 12.2 ms (11.5–13.6) | +2.5 ms | yes |
| Quality FAAA | `FXAA = 7` | 12.8 ms (11.7–13.9) | +3.0 ms | marginal |
| MSAA 4× | `MSAASamples = 1` | 21.1 ms (20.2–22.0) | +11.3 ms | no |
| MSAA 4× + Standard FXAA | `MSAASamples = 1`, `FXAA = 2` | 22.4 ms (21.6–23.3) | +12.7 ms | no |
| Supersampling 1.3× per axis, 2457² (PC-like) | `[PlayerVR] ResFactor = 0.3` | 15.1 ms (14.7–15.7) | +5.4 ms | no |
| Internal supersampling 1.3× | `AAFactor = 1.3` | 16.3 ms (15.3–20.1) | +6.5 ms | no |

Reading:

- **MSAA** more than doubles the frame. On a tile-based GPU MSAA is usually cheap because the samples never leave the on-chip tile
  memory, but the scene pass of this branch runs in direct (non-tiled) mode, the faster choice for its draw count (see *Foveated
  Rendering on the Steam Frame.md*): the 4× RGB16F buffer then goes through memory for every draw. It is not an option at 2160².
- **Supersampling** is the PC's remedy and costs 5–6 ms here: over the budget on a heavy table, affordable on light ones (Addams,
  Judge Dredd class, ≈ 5 ms baseline). `ResFactor` is the better of the two ways: the compositor does the downsampling in its own
  pass, while `AAFactor` adds a downsample pass to the application and showed one 20 ms outlier.
- **FXAA** is the only option within the budget. It is also surprisingly expensive for a post filter (2.5 ms here, 1.6–2.0 ms on a
  cool GPU, section 6.3, at 2 × 2160²): the
  tonemap pass then writes a full-resolution intermediate target that FXAA reads and writes again to the swapchain, instead of
  tonemapping straight into the swapchain. Fusing FXAA into the tonemap pass, or running it in a tiled pass, is a lead for later.
- None of the options addresses the shading aliasing of the chrome (section 3), hence the shader change.

## 5. Change 1: Standard FXAA by default on headset builds

`src/core/Settings_properties.inl`: the default of `Player/FXAA` is `2` (Standard FXAA) when `__STANDALONE__ && ENABLE_XR`, which is
the Steam Frame build (and the Windows MinGW OpenXR build, meant for a headset as well), and stays `0` elsewhere. A value set in the ini
wins, so the headset's ini keeps `FXAA = ` empty to follow the default. The desktop preview window of those builds gets FXAA too, which
is harmless.

## 6. Change 2: specular anti-aliasing in the material shader

Files: `src/shaders/bgfx/material.sh` (the technique and its use in `lightLoop`, shared by the basic material and the classic lights),
`src/shaders/bgfx/fs_ball.sc` (the ball's point-light highlights). Regenerated headers: `src/shaders/bgfx_basic.h`, `bgfx_ball.h`.

### 6.1 Technique

Kaplanyan et al., *Filtering Distributions of Normals for Shading Antialiasing* (HPG 2016) and Tokuyoshi & Kaplanyan, *Improved
Geometric Specular Antialiasing* (I3D 2019): the shading normal is not constant over the pixel; its distribution over the pixel's
footprint has a variance that can be estimated from the screen-space derivatives of the normal, and adding that variance to the
roughness of the BRDF lobe makes the pixel render the *average* highlight of its footprint instead of one sample of it.

In `material.sh`:

```glsl
#define SPECULAR_AA_PIXEL_SIGMA2 0.25 // Variance of the sample position inside the pixel: (half a pixel)^2
#define SPECULAR_AA_MAX_KERNEL 0.18   // Cap of the added roughness

vec2 NormalVariation(const vec3 N)          // x: variance of N over the pixel, y: squared change of N across one pixel (largest direction)
{
   const vec3 dNdx = dFdx(N);
   const vec3 dNdy = dFdy(N);
   const float dx2 = dot(dNdx, dNdx);
   const float dy2 = dot(dNdy, dNdy);
   return vec2(SPECULAR_AA_PIXEL_SIGMA2 * (dx2 + dy2), max(dx2, dy2));
}
float SpecularAAKernel(const vec2 v)        { return min(2.0 * v.x, SPECULAR_AA_MAX_KERNEL); }        // roughness (alpha^2) to add
float SpecularAAGlossyPower(const float n, const float k) { return 2.0 / (2.0 / (n + 2.0) + k) - 2.0; } // widened Blinn-Phong exponent
float SpecularAAMirrorMip(const vec2 v)     { return clamp(0.5 * log2(max(v.y, 1e-12)) + log2(envWidth / PI), 0.0, log2(envWidth) - 1.0); }
```

VPX describes a material's glossy lobe as a Blinn-Phong exponent `n = 2^(10 · roughness + 1)` (`Renderer.cpp:1091`,
`Roughness_WrapL_Edge_Thickness.x`), so the kernel is applied through the Beckmann-like equivalence `alpha² = 2 / (n + 2)`: convert,
add the kernel roughness, convert back. The widened exponent drives the two point lights (`DoPointLight`, whose normalisation
`(n + 1) / (8 · V·H)` keeps the energy) and the glossy environment lookup (`DoEnvmapGlossy`, whose mip level is derived from the
exponent).

The specular (clearcoat) layer does a mirror lookup of the environment map at mip 0 (`DoEnvmap2ndLayer`, formerly `texNoLod`), which
on a 4096-px map and a curved chrome part reads a different texel every frame. It now reads the mip whose texel matches the footprint of
the reflection: the reflection vector turns twice as fast as the normal, and the equirectangular map has `width / 2π` texels per radian,
so the footprint is `|dN| · width / π` texels and the mip is its `log2`. A flat surface (`|dN| = 0`) keeps mip 0, so flat mirrors are
unchanged.

### 6.2 Details that matter

- The derivatives are taken from the normal **before** `lightLoop` flips it for backside lighting (`N = -N` when `N·V < 0`): the flip is
  a per-pixel sign change that would read as an enormous variation along the line where it starts.
- They are taken after normal mapping, so the aliasing of high-frequency normal maps is filtered too.
- `dFdx`/`dFdy` need the four pixels of a quad to run the same code: `NormalVariation` is called at the top of `lightLoop` /
  `ballLightLoop`, before the material-dependent branches, and the callers only reach it through uniform branches
  (`#ifdef REFL`, `lightingOff`, `disableLighting`).
- The cap of 0.18 on the kernel (the papers' value) stops a noisy normal (a normal map at minification, or geometry at the horizon) from
  flattening the highlight to nothing: beyond it the surface is better served by a wide dull highlight than by none.
- The ball only widens its point-light highlights: its environment reflection already samples with an explicit LOD.
- The HLSL/GLSL shaders of the DirectX 9 / OpenGL renderers (`src/shaders/hlsl_glsl/`) are not changed; they are not built for the Frame.

### 6.3 Cost and verification

Same table and method as section 4, with the regenerated shaders, and a cool-down of 1–2.5 min between launches (see the note on
thermals below):

| Build | `FXAA` | GPU per frame |
|---|---|---|
| Before (section 4) | 0 | 9.7 ms (9.4–9.9) |
| Specular AA | 0 | **9.4 ms** (9.0–9.7), and 9.9 ms (9.1–10.6) in a hot run |
| Specular AA + Standard FXAA (the new defaults) | 2 (default) | **11.0 ms** (10.9–11.1) and 11.4 ms (11.1–11.9) |

The specular anti-aliasing costs nothing measurable (six derivative instructions and a dozen ALU per lit pixel). FXAA adds 1.6–2.0 ms
when the GPU is cool, which brings the defaults to ≈ 11 ms on this heavy table, 3 ms under the 72 Hz budget.

**Thermals.** The first run of the new defaults, launched right after a dozen back-to-back launches, measured 14.4 ms with the CPU
cores at 76–78 °C and the GPU at 67–70 °C before the launch (and 88–90 °C during a run, read from `/sys/class/thermal/thermal_zone*`
with type `gpuss-N-thermal`). After 2.5 min of rest (GPU at 60 °C) the same configuration measured 11.0 ms. The series of section 4 was
run back-to-back, so its later variants (MSAA, supersampling) are upper bounds; their ordering and the conclusion (MSAA 2× the frame,
supersampling over the budget) stand, FXAA's cost there (+2.5 ms) was inflated by heat. Measurements on the Frame need the
temperature logged and a rest between runs.

**Visual check** in the headset (table Ghostbusters VR room, head still, looking at the chrome ramps and wire guides): *to be
confirmed by the player*.

## 7. Per-table knobs

Settings are per table (the ini's `[Player]` / `[PlayerVR]` sections can be overridden per table from the in-game settings). The
tables differ a lot: the Ghostbusters VR room is 9.4 ms without AA, the Addams Family VR room (Bally 1992, v2.0) **13.5–15.1 ms
without AA and without specular AA**, i.e. at or over the 72 Hz budget by itself, which shows as judder on fast head movements. Its
breakdown (`VPX_GPU_PROFILE=1`): scene pass 10.4–11.7 ms, dynamic playfield reflection ("Playfield" probe) 1.6–1.7 ms, tonemap to the
swapchain 1.4–1.5 ms, bloom 0.2 ms.

- Tables under ≈ 10 ms without AA: keep the defaults (FXAA + specular AA), ≈ 11 ms.
- Tables at the budget (Addams class): the per-frame cost to cut is the scene pass, so lower the resolution for that table
  (`[PlayerVR] ResFactor = 0.21` ≈ 1728², the runtime's recommended size, 0.64× the pixels) or drop the dynamic playfield
  reflection (`PFReflection = 3`, "Static & Balls", −1.6 ms); FXAA then fits again.
- Tables with room to spare: `[PlayerVR] ResFactor = 0.3` (2457², +5.4 ms) gives the PC-like supersampled image; `0.33` (2703²)
  is the next step if the budget allows.
- Never `MSAASamples` at native resolution on the Frame.

A caution for measurements: VPX writes the ini back when a table closes, with the values it had loaded. An ini edited while a table
is running is overwritten at that table's exit (this reverted an `FXAA = 0` edit once, and the next runs measured FXAA on).

## 8. Dynamic resolution (2026-10-02)

Files: `src/renderer/RenderTarget.h/.cpp`, `RenderPass.h/.cpp`, `RenderFrame.h/.cpp`, `RenderDevice.h/.cpp`, `Renderer.cpp`, `RenderProbe.cpp`,
`Sampler.cpp`, `VRDevice.h/.cpp`, `src/core/Settings_properties.inl`, `src/ui/live/ingameui/VRSettingsPage.cpp`, `src/shaders/bgfx/fs_basic.sc`,
`fs_ball.sc` (headers regenerated).

The per-table knobs of section 7 are what a player would have to find by trial, and a table's cost changes with the view and with the heat
of the device (the same frame costs 2–3 ms more after ten minutes), so the resolution now follows the measured GPU time automatically.

### 8.1 How it works

- **Scaled rendering into the existing buffers.** The scene buffers keep their allocated size; a frame renders into their top left part at
  a scale `s` (`RenderDevice::BeginScaledRendering`): every render pass created in the frame carries the scale (`RenderPass::m_renderScale`),
  and `RenderTarget::Activate` sets the view rect (and the scissor of the area-of-interest passes) to `s × size` for the targets flagged
  `m_dynamicResolution` — scene buffers, post-process, bloom, AO, SSR, motion blur, the reflection and refraction probes, and the headset
  swapchain. Other targets (textures, ancillary windows, the irradiance precompute) are untouched. Nothing is reallocated, so the scale can
  change every frame.
- **Sampling the rendered part.** Full-screen passes read the source through `v_texcoord` 0..1: `DrawFullscreenTexturedQuad` and the two
  explicit quads (tonemap, bloom cut-off) use 0..`s` instead. The material shaders sample screen-space buffers with `gl_FragCoord × 1/size`,
  which maps to the rendered part by itself; the two lookups derived from clip space (refraction probe in `fs_basic.sc`, the ball's reflection
  of the previous frame in `fs_ball.sc`) are multiplied by the scale, passed in the free components `w_h_height.z` and
  `w_h_disableLighting.w`. The depth copy to the swapchain copies the rendered part.
- **Presentation.** After the frame is executed, the VR device reads the scale it was rendered with (`RenderFrame::m_outputRenderScale` →
  `RenderDevice::GetExecutedRenderScale`) and submits the projection layer (and its depth) with `imageRect` = the rendered part, so the
  compositor samples only that part of the swapchain image. The UI panel, the loading screen and captures are drawn after
  `EndScaledRendering` and keep the full size.
- **Controller** (`VRDevice::UpdateDynamicResolution`, render thread, once per frame after `xrWaitFrame`): reads the runtime's
  `/perfmetrics_meta/app/gpu_frametime` counter (`XR_META_performance_metrics`, now enabled whenever dynamic resolution is on) and compares
  it with the target, a fraction of the display's frame period. Over the target: the scale drops at once (2 % per frame, more when further
  over). Under 90 % of the target: it climbs by 0.3 % per frame (a second or two from the floor to full size). Clamped between the
  minimum setting and 100 %. A reading under 1 ms (nothing rendered, headset not worn) is ignored.
- **Budget**: the *shortest* predicted display period of the session, not the current one. SteamVR doubles the predicted period when it
  throttles an application to half rate after missed frames (27.8 ms reported at 72 Hz on the Frame): taken as the budget, it made the
  controller raise the resolution again and the application stayed throttled.

### 8.2 Settings (VR settings page, applied live, per table)

| Setting | Default | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `PlayerVR/DynamicResolution` | On (headset builds), Off elsewhere | The switch; Off renders at the table's `ResFactor` size |
| `PlayerVR/DynamicResolutionTarget` | 85 % | GPU time target as a fraction of the frame period (11.8 ms at 72 Hz) |
| `PlayerVR/DynamicResolutionMinScale` | 70 % | Lowest scale, per axis, of the table's resolution (1512² at 2160²; the Frame's recommended size is 80 %) |

A status line on the page and in the log every 5 s: `Dynamic resolution: 1728x1728 (80 %), GPU 11.2 ms of 13.9 ms (target 85 %)`.

### 8.3 First run on the Frame (Addams Family, FXAA + specular AA on)

The scale went from 100 % to the floor (then 80 %, the recommended size) within the first seconds and the GPU time settled at 10.8–11.5 ms
against the 11.8 ms target — the judder of section 7 gone at the start of the session. Ten minutes in, the same scale measured 13.2–14.7 ms
(heat), over the budget with no room left below the floor: hence the configurable minimum, defaulting to 70 %. Then the runtime throttled
the application to half rate, the predicted period doubled and the controller climbed back to 100 % (18 ms frames): hence the budget fix
above. Both are in the build; the run after them is reported below.

**Second run** (floor 70 %, budget fix), same table, GPU still warm from the first run, head moved as before: the scale went to the floor
(1518², 11.4 ms) in the first seconds, then climbed as the frame fell to 10.0 ms and settled in a **78–82 % band (1700–1780²) at
10.5–11.2 ms** for the rest of the run, with no step larger than 3 % between two status lines. The compositor's tally for the session:
7855 presents, **318 reprojected (4.0 %)**, against 24 % at a fixed 2160² and 10 % at a fixed 1720² (section 7). The budget stayed
13.9 ms throughout (no throttling this time).

## 9. Reproduction

On the headset (`ssh steamos@<frame>`), with the build deployed in `~/devkit-game/vpx_frame`:

```bash
cd ~/devkit-game/vpx_frame
INI=~/.local/share/VPinballX/10.8/VPinballX.ini
TABLE=~/Documents/VPinballX/Tables/<table>.vpx
# one variant: set the keys (empty = default), play, wait for frames, read the runtime's app GPU time
sed -i 's/^FXAA = .*/FXAA = 2/' $INI
LD_LIBRARY_PATH=$PWD DISPLAY=:1 VPX_XR_METRICS=1 ./VPinballX_BGFX -Play "$TABLE" > /tmp/run.out 2>&1 &
# ... wear the headset; the status line logs "gpu_frametime <ms>" every 5 s (0.18 ms = not rendering)
grep -o "gpu_frametime [0-9.]* ms" /tmp/run.out | tail -8
pkill -x VPinballX_BGFX
```

`VPX_GPU_PROFILE=1` instead of `VPX_XR_METRICS=1` adds the per-target breakdown ("GPU by target") to tell the scene pass from the post
passes.
